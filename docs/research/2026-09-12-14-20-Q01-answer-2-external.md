# Q01: Agent-First Coordination via GitHub Issues and gh CLI

Bottom line up front: for a co-located team of five whose heterogeneous
AI coding agents coordinate only through GitHub Issues and the gh CLI in
a sub-four-hour sprint, Issues + gh is the correct substrate, but the v1
protocol is over-built. Roughly two thirds of its ceremony is theater at
this timescale and should be demoted to optional; what genuinely earns
its keep is a small kernel: claim-with-a-plan, contract-before-code on
the named seams, file-glob ownership per workstream, a pinned sync hub,
and small PRs the owner merges. Everything below distinguishes claims
backed by (i) primary sources, (ii) practitioner consensus, and (iii)
reasoning by analogy from distributed systems and human teams, and flags
where evidence is thin.

A note on evidence quality up front: there is almost no direct, published,
peer-reviewed measurement of LLM agents coordinating specifically through
GitHub Issues under time pressure. The strongest primary source on agent
teams is the MAST failure taxonomy (empirical, 1,642 traces); the
strongest primary sources on the coordination mechanics are distributed
systems papers (Chubby, Kleppmann) and GitHub's own docs. A large part of
this report is therefore reasoning by analogy, and that is called out
explicitly wherever it applies.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 1) Executive summary (verdict + three highest-leverage changes)

VERDICT: Keep Issues + gh as the only channel. It is the right call for
a heterogeneous fleet that cannot share a runtime, and it gives you a
durable, queryable, append-only ledger, which is exactly what agents that
cannot hear the table need. But cut the ceremony hard. With about 100
minutes left, protocol elaboration is a liability, not an asset.

The three highest-leverage changes:

1. Collapse the 15-verb vocabulary to a 6-verb sprint set (CLAIM, DONE,
   BLOCKED, CONTRACT, ACCEPT, SYNC). Every extra verb is surface area for
   cross-harness parse divergence and for agents optimizing compliance
   over shipping.

2. Fix the lock semantics. Assignment-as-lock plus earliest-CLAIM
   tiebreak is an efficiency lock, not a correctness lock, because it has
   no fencing token (Kleppmann, primary source). Shorten the lease to 20
   minutes and make the shared repo, not the lock, the place where
   correctness is enforced (small PRs + owner merge is your fence).

3. Declare file-glob ownership per workstream up front (Conway's law).
   Map the six workstreams to non-overlapping globs so the overlap check
   becomes the single most valuable primitive on the board. This is the
   cheapest merge-conflict prevention available and the one most likely
   to save the demo.

Survives as core: claim-with-a-plan; contract-before-code on the named
seams; file-glob ownership; pinned sync hub; small PRs to main.
Theater at this timescale: negotiation verbs beyond CONTRACT/ACCEPT;
mandatory 30-minute heartbeats; DEP-DONE automation; the full type and
priority taxonomies.

## 3) Sprint-minimum protocol vs full protocol, with switch condition

### Sprint-minimum (use this today)

Verbs (6): CLAIM, DONE, BLOCKED, CONTRACT (replaces PROPOSE), ACCEPT,
SYNC. Keep the bold-verb header shape exactly as locked:
`**VERB** | agent: <producer/version> | human: @<login> | at: <ISO-8601 UTC>`.

Labels (minimal state machine):
- `ws:<workstream>` (the six values, unchanged).
- `status:{claimed, blocked, review}` (three values; "unclaimed" is the
  absence of an assignee, "in-progress" collapses into "claimed",
  "in-review" becomes "review").
- `p0` as a single binary label meaning on the demo golden path.
- `contract` for the seam issues.
- `needs-human` for escalation.
- `hub` for the one pinned coordination issue.

Rituals: claim-with-a-plan; contract-before-code on the seams only; one
SYNC at session start and one at the mid-point integration checkpoint;
owner merges small PRs continuously.

### Full protocol (the v1 document, essentially)

Fifteen verbs, five-value status, priority p0-p2, the type taxonomy
{task, contract, decision, bug}, DEP-DONE automation, the four-round
negotiation cap, and mandatory 30-minute heartbeats. This is a reasonable
design for a longer-lived, larger, or post-hackathon-maintained project.

### Switch condition (make it explicit and mechanical)

Use sprint-minimum when ANY of: time-to-deadline < ~6 hours, OR team
<= ~6 agents, OR the artifact is a throwaway demo/prototype. Switch up to
full when ALL of the throwaway conditions fail, i.e. the artifact will be
maintained after the event AND the team exceeds ~8 agents OR the number
of cross-slice seams exceeds the number of people (so contracts need real
lifecycle tracking). This threshold is reasoning by analogy [A] from
Kanban (process weight should scale with WIP and lifetime) and from the
trunk-based literature (tooling like merge queues only pays off above
roughly 5-20 PRs/day). It is not from a controlled study on agents.

## 4) Parameters with justification

### Stale-lock timeout: 20 minutes (down from 45)

Reasoning [A], anchored to primary sources. A lease should be a small
fraction of the system's lifetime so a dead holder is reclaimed while the
work still matters. Google's Chubby (Burrows, OSDI 2006, [P]) extends the
session lease by a fixed default of 12 seconds, so KeepAlives are
typically exchanged about every 12 seconds, with a 45-second grace period
on expiry, and it is explicitly a coarse-grained service where locks are
held for minutes to hours. In a 4-hour sprint, a 45-minute lease is about
19% of the entire event, which is far too long to notice and reclaim a
crashed or wandered-off agent. Twenty minutes is about 8% of the window
and still comfortably spans one normal work chunk plus a missed check.

Critical correctness caveat [P]: assignment-as-lock has no fencing token.
Kleppmann ("How to do distributed locking", 2016) distinguishes locks
"for efficiency" from locks where "correctness depends on the lock," and
warns that a lock scheme that "lacks a facility for generating fencing
tokens" cannot protect a shared resource "against long delays in the
network or in paused processes." An LLM agent that pauses (long tool
call, rate-limit backoff, human stepping away) can believe it still owns
an issue after its lease has been reclaimed and another agent has taken
it. Do not try to build fencing tokens into Issues; that is over-engineering
for a sprint. Instead, put correctness where it belongs: the shared repo.
Small PRs plus owner-merge means two agents can both think they own a
slice, but only one merge lands, and the owner sees the collision at
review time. Treat the issue lock as an efficiency lock (avoid wasted
duplicate work) and treat main + review as the correctness fence.

### Heartbeat cadence: 20-30 minutes, but soft/optional

The real liveness signal is commit and PR activity plus claim recency,
not a manually posted STATUS. Requiring a manual heartbeat verb invites
exactly the compliance-over-shipping pathology that MAST documents.
Detect staleness from `updatedAt` on the issue and from PR pushes, and
only fall back to a manual STATUS expectation if an agent's harness
cannot push commits frequently. Evidence: [A] plus [PC] from trunk-based
practice where frequent small commits are the observable pulse.

### Negotiation-round cap: 2 rounds, then needs-human (down from 4)

Infinite or ping-pong negotiation between agents is a recognized
LLM-team failure. Because the humans are literally at the same table, the
escalation ladder should be short: propose, one counter, then stop and
tag a human. Google SRE guidance ([P], SRE book/workbook) is that when an
issue cannot be bounded quickly you escalate early rather than let a P2
drift into a P1; the analogous rule here is to escalate on the second
unresolved round rather than the fourth. Four rounds is defensible only
in the full protocol where humans may be remote and asynchronous.

### Per-agent claim limit: WIP = 1 (down from 2)

Little's law ([P] as a queueing result; [A] in application): average
cycle time equals work-in-progress divided by throughput, so for fixed
throughput, lower WIP means shorter cycle time. With five agents and a
single short window you want work finished and merged, not many slices
left half-done at the buzzer. WIP=1 also makes the board trivially
legible: one agent, one active issue. Allow a transient second claim only
to pick up the next issue in the same second you post DONE on the current
one.

## 5) Failure-modes-and-defenses table (LLM-agent teams on GitHub)

The failure categories below are grounded in MAST (Cemri et al., "Why Do
Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail?", arXiv:2503.13657, NeurIPS 2025 [P]),
built from 1,642 annotated traces across 7 frameworks with inter-annotator
agreement Cohen's kappa = 0.88. MAST's headline finding matters here: the
authors conclude that "improvements in the base model capabilities will
be insufficient to address the full MAST," and that a well-designed system
on the same model can succeed where a badly organized one fails. In other
words, protocol design, not model choice, is the lever. Category shares:
System Design / Specification issues 41.77%, Inter-Agent Misalignment
36.94%, Task Verification 21.30%.

| Failure mode | Maps to MAST | Defense (rule / script / human ritual) | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Duplicated work | Inter-agent misalignment | assignment-as-lock + earliest-CLAIM tiebreak + startup dedupe query | [A]+[PC] |
| Hallucinated / guessed interfaces | FM-1.1 disobey task spec; specification cluster 41.77% | contract-before-code on seams; build only after ACCEPT | [P] MAST |
| Infinite / ping-pong negotiation | FM-2.x misalignment | 2-round cap then needs-human | [A] SRE escalation |
| Silent stalls | FM-1.5 unaware of termination; FM-1.3 step repetition | 20-min lease + activity-based liveness | [P] modes, [A] timeout |
| Over-claiming | inter-agent misalignment | WIP=1 | [A] Little's law |
| Premature "done" | FM-3.1 premature termination (6.2%); FM-3.2 incomplete verification (8.2%) | Done-when checkboxes + owner review gate | [P] MAST; adding a verification step raised ChatDev success by 15.6 points |
| Context loss on handoff | FM-1.4 loss of conversation history (~2.8%) | everything material written into the issue, never only said aloud | [P] MAST |
| Rate-limit storms vs GitHub API | system-design | polling floor >= 60s; server-side --jq filters; back off on 429 | [P] GitHub docs |

On rate limits [P]: GitHub's REST API allows 5,000 requests per hour for
an authenticated user, and the secondary limits state "No more than 100
concurrent requests are allowed. This limit is shared across the REST API
and GraphQL API," plus a per-endpoint budget of 900 points per minute for
reads. Five agents each polling the board every few seconds in a tight
loop can trip the secondary concurrency and per-endpoint limits long
before the hourly 5,000 is exhausted, and continuing to hammer after a
429 risks a temporary ban. So the binding constraint is burst behavior,
not the hourly total. Set a polling floor of at least 60 seconds, fetch
the whole board in one `gh issue list --json` call rather than per-issue
calls, and honor `retry-after` / `x-ratelimit-reset`.

## 6) Integration cadence for five streams in under four hours

Branch per issue, small PRs into main, the owner merges. This is the
trunk-based pattern and it is [PC]: short-lived branches, frequent small
merges, and integration-as-you-go rather than a big-bang merge at the
end. The trunk-based literature is blunt that long-lived branches invert
every benefit they were meant to provide, and that branch lifetime should
track shipping frequency; in a 4-hour sprint a branch should live tens of
minutes, not the whole event.

Shared "kernel" files are the danger zone. In this project the kernel is
the set of files several slices must touch: the window opener API, the
chat tool-handler registry, and the filesystem API type definitions.
Conway's law ([P], Conway 1968) predicts that seams needing coordination
harden into interfaces exactly where communication is expensive, and here
the agents cannot talk across the table, so the interface must be made
explicit or it will be guessed wrong. Handle kernel files by:
(a) declaring each as a `contract` issue with the interface written out;
(b) assigning a single owner per kernel file so there is one writer;
(c) stubbing the interface early so dependent slices build against the
stub and integrate against the real thing later.

Is the explicit integration checkpoint worth its cost? Yes, once. A
single checkpoint at roughly the 50% mark (about T-minus-2 hours here)
where every open PR is merged or explicitly deferred, and the demo path
is smoke-tested end to end, is worth its ~15 minutes. The GitButler /
Scott Chacon experience rebuilding Git in Rust with agents is the closest
practitioner datapoint [PC]: a parallel long-running agent broke shared
test-harness state and nearly sank the project because uncoordinated
parallel writes cascaded; the lesson drawn was worktree isolation plus
more frequent merge checkpoints would have contained the blast radius. A
second checkpoint is not worth it at four hours; it becomes a stand-up
ritual that competes with shipping.

## 7) What must be written down vs. said aloud

The governing rule: if an agent that was not in the room would get it
wrong without a piece of information, that information MUST be in an
Issue. Everything else can be said aloud.

Must be written (into Issues):
- Interface shapes and contracts (the seam APIs).
- Done-when criteria for each issue.
- File-glob ownership and any change to it.
- Blockers and their cause, and what would unblock.
- Any decision an absent agent must honor (an ADR-style note, MADR 4.0
  format for anything architectural).

Can be said aloud (humans only):
- Encouragement, "are you stuck?" nudges, merge timing.
- Whose turn it is to grab coffee.

This is a direct application of MAST's loss-of-conversation-history and
information-withholding modes [P]: the agents' only shared memory is the
repo, so anything spoken and not written is, from the agents' point of
view, information withheld.

## 9) Cross-harness parsability and identity (part b, made concrete)

Design assignment-as-lock, labels-as-state, and structured comments so a
Claude Code agent, a "Pi" harness, and a Cursor-style IDE agent can all
read and write the ledger, including agents that cannot run shell scripts.

- Identity: the header's `human: @<login>` field is your actor
  disambiguator. When two humans run the same model (both "Claude Code"),
  the `agent:` field will collide but the GitHub login will not, because
  each gh is authenticated as its own human with write access. Always key
  ownership on the GitHub assignee/login, never on the model string.
  Treat `agent:<producer/version>` as advisory metadata and `@<login>`
  as the identity of record. This is the one identity rule that must hold.

- Parsability: keep the header a single line with fixed `key: value`
  fields separated by ` | `, and keep the first token a bold verb so a
  no-code agent can match on the literal prefix `**CLAIM**`. Agents that
  can run gh --jq parse structurally; agents that cannot can still string-
  match the first line. Do not nest structure the no-code path cannot
  reproduce by hand.

- State: labels are the authoritative state machine, comments are the
  log. An agent reconstructs state from labels + assignee (one query),
  and reads intent from the last few comments. This mirrors MetaGPT's
  shared message pool ([P], arXiv:2308.00352): "we introduce a shared
  message pool that allows all agents to exchange messages directly ...
  publish their structured messages in the pool [and] access messages
  from other entities transparently." Your hub issue plus labels IS that
  pool; the difference is you get it for free from GitHub instead of
  building a broker.

## 10) Concrete gh / gh --jq recipes

List unclaimed p0 issues (golden-path work available now):
```
gh issue list --label p0 --search "no:assignee" --state open \
  --json number,title,labels --jq \
  '.[] | "\(.number)\t\(.title)"'
```

Detect stale claims (no update in 20 minutes) among claimed issues:
```
gh issue list --label status:claimed --state open \
  --json number,title,updatedAt,assignees --jq \
  '.[] | select((now - (.updatedAt|fromdateiso8601)) > 1200) |
   "\(.number)\tSTALE\t\(.assignees[0].login // "none")\t\(.title)"'
```
(1200 seconds = 20 minutes; `now` is jq's current epoch.)

Find file-glob overlaps between two claims (run at claim time; compares
the "Files" line an agent posts against every other open claim). Simplest
version: list each open issue's Files field and eyeball/grep:
```
gh issue list --state open --json number,body --jq \
  '.[] | {n:.number,
          files:(.body|capture("Files:(?<f>[^\n]*)").f // "")}'
```
Then grep the candidate glob against that output before claiming. A
zero-overlap result is your green light.

Read the last 5 SYNC lines from the hub issue (say hub is #1):
```
gh issue view 1 --json comments --jq \
  '[.comments[].body | select(startswith("**SYNC**"))][-5:] | .[]'
```

Check for duplicate claims by the same agent (WIP>1 violations):
```
gh issue list --label status:claimed --state open \
  --json number,assignees --jq \
  'group_by(.assignees[0].login)[] |
   select(length > 1) |
   "\(.[0].assignees[0].login) holds \(map(.number)|join(","))"'
```

Board snapshot at session start (own claims, staleness, needs-human):
```
gh issue list --state open \
  --json number,title,labels,assignees,updatedAt --jq \
  '.[] | "\(.number)\t\([.labels[].name]|join(","))\t\(.title)"'
```

Rate-limit safe polling: sleep at least 60s between board refreshes,
prefer one `gh issue list --json` over N `gh issue view` calls, and stop
on HTTP 429 until `retry-after` elapses.

## 11) Common pitfalls and anti-patterns (and how to detect each on the board)

- Protocol theater: agents posting well-formed verbs while no PRs land.
  Detect: rising comment count with flat merged-PR count. If SYNC lines
  accumulate but `gh pr list --state merged` does not grow, you are
  optimizing compliance over shipping. This is the skeptic's core warning
  and it is real: MAST shows failures are organizational, so a heavier
  protocol does not buy correctness by itself [P].
- Ghost locks: an issue is `status:claimed` but its assignee's agent
  died. Detect: the stale-claim query above.
- Guessed interfaces: a slice ships against an interface that was never
  put on a `contract` issue. Detect: PRs touching kernel files with no
  linked ACCEPTed contract issue. This is the highest-cost failure
  because it is invisible until integration.
- Over-claiming: the duplicate-claims query returns any agent with >1.
- Premature done: DONE posted with unchecked Done-when boxes. Detect:
  grep the issue body for unchecked `- [ ]` at DONE time.
- Board talk substituting for repo writes: a decision only in the hub
  chatter, never captured as a contract or ADR. Detect: needs-human or
  BLOCKED bodies that reference "as we discussed" with no issue link.
- Rate-limit storm: 429s in agent logs. Detect: agents silently falling
  behind the board because their polling is being throttled.

## 12) Perspectives (implementer, practitioner, skeptic, theorist)

Implementer: ship the v1.1 diff in section 8 and the recipes in section
10. The concrete wins are WIP=1, the 20-minute lease, the six-verb set,
and the file-glob map. Those are one-line changes to the v1 doc.

Practitioner: the honest field reports are that process gets abandoned
under time pressure and that the survivors are ownership boundaries and
frequent integration. The Claude Code worktree playbooks [PC] converge on
"hard isolation so agents do not need to coordinate," and Boris Cherny,
who created Claude Code, has publicly called running several worktree-
isolated sessions in parallel the single biggest productivity unlock.
You cannot use worktrees to isolate five different humans' machines, so
your file-glob ownership map is the substitute: it is isolation declared
in the ledger instead of enforced by the filesystem. The GitButler/Grit
project [PC] is the cautionary tale: uncoordinated parallel writes broke
shared state and nearly killed the project, and the fix was tighter task
boundaries and merge checkpoints, not more chat.

Skeptic: with 100 minutes left, the case for cutting most of this is
strong. Issue-based coordination is strictly worse than a shout across
the table for anything urgent, low-stakes, and human-to-human. The only
reason to write anything down is that the AGENTS cannot hear you. So the
skeptic's rule is: humans talk freely; the ledger exists solely so agents
stay correct. Anything in the protocol that does not directly keep an
absent agent correct is cuttable today. The genuine risk, backed by MAST
[P], is that a rich protocol makes agents look busy and coordinated while
the demo does not converge.

Theorist: the right mental model is a shared ledger providing optimistic
concurrency at low contention. With five agents on six well-separated
slices, write contention is low, so optimistic control (claim, proceed,
detect conflict at merge) beats pessimistic locking, which the OCC
literature supports for low-conflict workloads [PC/P]. Assignment is an
efficiency lease, not a fenced lock (Kleppmann [P]); the merge step is
the linearization point. Conway's law [P] explains why the seams need
contracts: interfaces harden where communication is expensive, and here
the agent-to-agent channel is expensive by construction. Little's law
motivates WIP=1. SRE escalation design motivates the short 2-round ladder.

## 13) Assessment, confidence, surprises, open questions

Assessment: Issues-based coordination is the right substrate here, at the
sprint-minimum ceremony level. The v1 protocol is a good full-protocol
design mis-applied to a 4-hour window. Adopt v1.1.

Confidence: MEDIUM. Here is exactly how much rests on what. The failure
taxonomy and the "design beats model" conclusion are primary-source and
solid [P: MAST]. The lock semantics critique, the lease-fraction
reasoning, and the OCC/Conway/Little arguments are primary sources in
their own domains applied here by analogy [P for the source, A for the
transfer]. The specific parameter values (20 minutes, 2 rounds, WIP=1)
are defensible by analogy but are NOT measured on agents coordinating via
GitHub Issues; that direct evidence does not appear to exist publicly.
The practitioner claims (worktrees, trunk-based, GitButler) are consistent
but are blog-grade [PC], not controlled studies. So the shape of the
recommendation is high-confidence; the exact numbers are medium-to-low.

What would change the conclusion: a controlled measurement of coordination
overhead and duplicate-work rate for heterogeneous agents on GitHub Issues
at different ceremony levels. If such data showed that heavier structure
raised throughput, section 8's REMOVE list would shrink. If it showed the
lease length was irrelevant compared to merge discipline, parameter
tuning would matter even less than argued here.

Surprising / non-obvious findings:
- Ceremony can reduce throughput. Because MAST shows failures are
  organizational rather than model-driven, adding protocol does not buy
  correctness and can actively induce compliance-over-shipping behavior.
  This is counterintuitive if you expect "more coordination = safer."
- The single most valuable primitive is the least glamorous one:
  file-glob ownership. It out-earns the entire elegant verb grammar.
- Contract-before-code is the one heavyweight ritual that clearly pays for
  itself, because specification issues are the largest MAST category
  (41.77%) and adding a verification/contract step measurably raised
  success in MAST's own interventions (ChatDev +15.6 points).
- Assignment-as-lock is weaker than it looks: with no fencing token it is
  only an efficiency lock, so the demo's real safety comes from
  owner-merged small PRs, not from the lock protocol at all.

Open questions:
- What is the actual duplicate-claim rate for five heterogeneous agents on
  one repo, and does earliest-CLAIM tiebreak resolve same-second races in
  practice or just in theory?
- Do no-code (bash-free) harnesses reliably reproduce the header line by
  hand under time pressure, or does the fallback degrade first?
- Is one integration checkpoint enough, or does the optimum scale with the
  number of kernel files rather than with time?
